III.08

Saga Pattern

WHAT IS IT?
FUNDAMENTAL IDEA

A long-running business transaction split into local transactions across services, with compensating actions to undo completed steps on failure.

Replace distributed ACID transactions with a sequence of local commits and explicit compensations.

PROBLEM IT SOLVES
WHY IT WAS INTRODUCED

Maintaining consistency across services that own separate databases without two-phase commit.

Microservices with database-per-service cannot use a single transaction; sagas (Garcia-Molina, 1987) provide eventual consistency.

How it works

The saga starts and the first local transaction commits.
Each step triggers the next via command or event.
If a step fails, the saga stops moving forward.
Compensating transactions undo earlier steps in reverse order.
Saga state is persisted so it can recover from crashes.
All steps and compensations must be idempotent.

Advantages

Disadvantages

Consistency without distributed locks or 2PC.

+ Services stay autonomous and available.

Only eventual consistency; no isolation between steps.

+ Works with heterogeneous data stores.

Compensation logic is complex to design.

+ Explicit business-level failure handling.

Hard to reason about and test failure paths.
Intermediate states are visible to others.

When to use

When not to use

Multi-service business processes such as orders, bookings and payments where each service owns its data.

Operations needing strict atomicity and isolation; if two things always change together, keep them in one service.

Real-world applications

Key engineering considerations

E-commerce checkout
Travel booking (flight + hotel + car)
Money transfer between ledgers.

Choose orchestration or choreography per saga. Use semantic locks to limit anomalies. Persist state, use timeouts and make every step idempotent. See Orchestration vs Choreography (Part IX).
