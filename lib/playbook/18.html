DEEP DIVE

The road network is a directed weighted graph: intersections are nodes, road segments are edges, and the weight is the predicted travel time. An ETA is the cost of the shortest path between two points.

Why plain Dijkstra is not enough Dijkstra works, but exploring a continent-sized graph for every request is far too slow at Uber's request rate. The standard answer is preprocessing: spend effort offline to build a structure that makes each online query touch only a tiny part of the graph. Techniques in this family include graph partitioning, contraction hierarchies and A* with landmarks. Uber has described partitioning the graph and precomputing routes within and between partitions.

Making weights realistic

Edge weights come from live and historical traffic, usually per road segment and time of day.
A model (often gradient-boosted trees or a neural network) can correct the raw routing estimate using features such as weather, events and time. The route engine gives a baseline; ML closes the gap.

Map matching GPS points are noisy and jump off the road. Map matching snaps a sequence of points to the most likely path along the road graph. A Hidden Markov Model decoded with the Viterbi algorithm balances “close to the GPS reading” against “a plausible move from the previous road”. A Kalman filter smooths position and speed estimates. Clean matched traces feed both live ETA and the traffic data.

TA K E AWAY

When an algorithm is too slow online, move the work offline. When data is noisy, model the uncertainty instead of trusting a single reading.
