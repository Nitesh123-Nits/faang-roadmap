THE PAPER IN ONE LINE

Combine a messaging API with an append-only, partitioned log, so one system can feed both real-time and offline consumers at high throughput.

IN EVERYDAY TERMS

A notebook where people only ever write at the bottom of the page. Each reader keeps their own bookmark and reads at their own pace. Nobody erases lines, and a reader who lost their place can simply go back to an earlier page. Kafka is that notebook, spread over many machines.

THE PROBLEM

LinkedIn produced a flood of log data: page views, searches, ad impressions and operational metrics, roughly a billion messages a day. Enterprise messaging systems (ActiveMQ, RabbitMQ) focus on rich delivery guarantees, not raw throughput. Log aggregators of the time were built for offline loading into Hadoop. Kafka was designed to be both: a distributed log that real-time consumers and batch systems can read.

Topics are split into partitions and consumed independently by consumer groups.

A topic is split into partitions, each a log stored on a broker. Producers append; consumers pull.
Messages are addressed by offset (position in the log), so no random-access index is needed.
A consumer group gets each message once. A partition is read by only one consumer of a group at a time, so partition count caps parallelism.
Brokers are stateless about consumers. The consumer owns its offset, so it can rewind and replay. Retention is time-based (typically 7 days).
HOW IT WORKS

Why it is fast: boring on purpose

Sequential disk Log segments of roughly 1 GB; appends and reads are sequential, which disks and the OS handle very well.
OS page cache No application-level cache. Kafka relies on the OS page cache and avoids double buffering.

Zero-copy sendfile copies from the file to the socket inside the kernel, skipping two copies and a system call.

Batching Producers batch sends and consumers pull up to a byte limit.

Design bet

Simple, boring, OS-friendly I/O beats clever in-process caching.

Coordination and guarantees

ZooKeeper holds broker and consumer registries and the persistent offset registry. Consumers rebalance partitions among themselves.
At-least-once delivery. After a consumer crash, some messages may be delivered again, so applications must de-duplicate.
Ordering only within a partition. There is no global order across partitions.
Each message carries a CRC to detect corruption.

Then vs. now

The 2011 paper lists replication as future work; it is a core feature of Kafka today, and newer versions also offer idempotent producers and transactions. KRaft mode replaces ZooKeeper with a Raft-based quorum (see Chapter 3). Use the paper for the why, and the current docs for the how.

WHAT IT GIVES UP
No per-message routing or priorities, and no broker-side tracking of who has read what.
Consumer-group rebalances pause consumption, and a hot partition key limits throughput.
USE IT IN YOUR WORK

Your key is your ordering and your parallelism. Same key → same partition → ordered. Size partitions for your maximum consumer parallelism. With Spring Kafka, commit offsets after processing and make handlers idempotent. Replaying from an offset then becomes a feature, not a risk.

“Kafka is a partitioned, replicated commit log: sequential I/O, page cache and zero-copy make it fast, consumers pull and track offsets, and ordering exists only within a partition.”
